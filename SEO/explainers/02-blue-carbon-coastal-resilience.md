**Blue carbon is the carbon captured and stored by coastal and marine ecosystems such as mangroves.** Mangroves store up to ten times more carbon per hectare than forests on land. They also stabilise coastlines, shield communities from storms and rising seas, and support fishing and tourism. Protecting them helps people adapt to climate change, not just cut emissions.

## Why do coastal ecosystems matter for climate adaptation?

Mangroves stabilise coastlines, reduce erosion and protect communities from storms and rising seas. They provide food, timber and tourism income, and act as nurseries for marine life.

Coral reefs cover less than 1 percent of the seafloor but support at least 25 percent of marine species. They give coastal communities food, economic security and protection from storms.

## What threatens them?

Coastal development, aquaculture and deforestation have destroyed nearly half of the world’s mangroves. Pollution, rising temperatures and rising seas add pressure.

An estimated 14 percent of the world’s coral reefs were lost between 2009 and 2018, and about half of global coral reef cover has been lost since the 1950s, through ocean warming and acidification, stronger storms, pollution and overfishing.

## How can blue carbon pay for conservation?

Communities that protect or restore mangroves can sell carbon credits and use the income for local priorities. On Kenya’s coast, near Gazi Bay and Vanga, [Plan Vivo](/partner-stories/plan-vivo/)-certified projects do exactly that ([read the field visit](/news/generating-revenue-from-conservation-to-build-resilience-in-coastal-kenya/)).

But many blue carbon projects never get past the early stages. They lack technical expertise, upfront funding and support to attract investors. Blue carbon’s share of the voluntary carbon market has only grown from 0.2% to 0.35% in recent years.

The [Blue Catalyst Fund](/partner-stories/blue-catalyst-fund/) tackles that bottleneck. It is a not-for-profit, recoverable grant facility that takes early-stage mangrove projects to investment readiness. Projects must show that local communities are genuine partners in design, decisions and benefit-sharing. Successful projects repay their grants, so the money is recycled into future projects.

## What about reefs and island states?

Marine protected areas give reefs a chance to recover. [Blue Alliance](/partner-stories/blue-alliance/) co-manages marine protected areas covering more than 1.7 million hectares in Indonesia, the Philippines and Tanzania, and reinvests profits from “reef-positive” businesses such as ecotourism and aquaculture.

Small Island Developing States steward vast ocean areas but receive only 0.2% of global climate finance. The [Outrigger Impact Fund](/partner-stories/outrigger/) invests in island businesses in blue carbon, ecotourism, fisheries management and ecosystem restoration.

## How does the Trafigura Foundation support blue carbon?

Through its [Thriving nature](/area-of-work/thriving-nature/) area of work, which supports mangrove management, community-led ecosystem management and payments for ecosystem services. Partner targets include:

- **Blue Catalyst Fund (by 2035):** over 150,000 hectares of coastal ecosystem protected or restored, more than 324,000 people positively affected, and USD 282 million in benefits shared directly with communities.
- **Blue Alliance (by 2028):** expand co-management to 4 million hectares and improve the livelihoods and food security of about 32,000 coastal community members.

## Frequently asked questions

**What counts as a blue carbon ecosystem?**
Coastal ecosystems that store carbon, mainly mangroves. **[client to confirm: add seagrass and salt marshes only if the Foundation wants to cover them; the site currently mentions mangroves and seagrass meadows.]**

**Are blue carbon credits good for local communities?**
They can be, when communities share in the design, decisions and revenue. Blue Catalyst Fund and Plan Vivo both require that.

**Is blue carbon about mitigation or adaptation?**
Both. Mangroves store carbon, and they also protect coasts from storms and support livelihoods.
